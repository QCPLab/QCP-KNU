Methods

Animal in vivo pharmacokinetics

Concentration–time profiles in rats and mice are a starting point of much of our work. We give a drug candidate or a marketed drug by one or more routes, collect blood at planned times and, when the question requires it, tissues, urine, bile or feces, and measure the drug and its metabolites by LC-MS/MS.

Typical workflow

  1. Design the study: species and strain, dose levels, routes (for example intravenous and oral), fasted or fed state and the sampling schedule.
  2. Dose the animals and collect blood serially; collect brain or other tissues, urine, bile and feces when distribution or excretion is the question.
  3. Quantify the parent drug and its metabolites in each sample with a validated LC-MS/MS method.
  4. Calculate exposure and disposition parameters (AUC, clearance, volume of distribution, half-life, bioavailability) by non-compartmental analysis, or fit compartmental models.
  5. Compare doses, sexes, feeding states, formulations and disease models to see what changes exposure.

In vitro pharmacokinetics and metabolism

Experiments outside the animal explain what we see inside it: how quickly and by which routes a drug is metabolized, which metabolites form, how well it crosses membranes, and which enzymes and transporters it meets.

Typical workflow

  1. Incubate the drug with liver microsomes or other enzyme sources, follow its disappearance and the metabolites that appear by LC-MS/MS, and identify the metabolites from their fragment ions.
  2. Measure the kinetics of metabolism and of transport, not only a single time point.
  3. Measure membrane permeability in vitro and relate it to the brain uptake and concentrations seen in vivo.
  4. Test uptake and inhibition of drug transporters such as OAT1, OATP1B1 and the organic cation transporters, to judge the risk of drug–drug interactions.
  5. Profile the physicochemical properties of candidate compounds with validated screening methods.
  6. Read the in vitro results together with the animal data, as in our work on lobeglitazone metabolites in rat liver microsomes and their formation in vivo.

LC-MS/MS bioanalysis

Every pharmacokinetic result starts with a reliable concentration. We develop and validate liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) assays for drugs and their metabolites in plasma, serum and tissue, and apply them to study samples.

Typical workflow

  1. Tune the mass spectrometer for the analyte and an internal standard and choose the ion transitions for multiple reaction monitoring.
  2. Develop the sample preparation and the chromatographic separation so that the run is clean, sensitive and fast.
  3. Validate the method: selectivity, linearity of the calibration curve, accuracy and precision, recovery, matrix effect and stability.
  4. Run the study samples together with calibration standards and quality-control samples in every batch.
  5. Hand the concentrations to the pharmacokinetic analysis. For selected analytes we have also used GC-MS or HPLC-UV.

How a population PK model is fitted

About the two interactive explainers: they are teaching examples built on simulated patients and public molecule structures, not results from patients or from our experiments. In the deep-learning example the molecules are real public structures and their logP is calculated by software, not measured. The calculations run in your browser; nothing is sent anywhere.

People differ in how fast they clear a drug. A nonlinear mixed-effects (NLME) model describes the typical values and the spread between individuals at once. The optimizer starts from poor guesses and improves them step by step; here you can move through every step.

  1. Choose a sampling scenario: rich, moderate or sparse.
  2. Press play, or drag the slider through the optimizer's iterations.
  3. Watch the fitted curves, the parameter estimates and the objective function change.
  4. Select a subject to compare its data with its individual fit.

Simulated data from 30 virtual subjects and a single model. The estimator is a from-scratch implementation of first-order conditional estimation with interaction (FOCE-I). It illustrates the idea and is not a substitute for validated software.

How a neural network computes

A neural network is a chain of simple calculations: weighted sums followed by a non-linearity. Training changes the weights to reduce the error. Run the forward pass, inspect the gradients of the backward pass, and follow message passing across a real drug molecule.

Teaching examples with small models. The neural network learns a known formula from synthetic patients. The graph network learns to reproduce the Crippen logP that RDKit calculates (a calculated value, not a measurement) from a set of about 4,600 public structures (about 3,200 of them used for training) (NCI Open Database, as shipped with RDKit). They show the mechanics and are not validated for prediction.
